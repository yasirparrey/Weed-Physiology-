## 5.1 Why preference tuning

**Context: the model may misbehave, and we need to inject negative signals.**

The lecture's example. Prompt: *"Suggest a new activity I could do with my teddy
bear."* An SFT-tuned model might reply:

> *"I'd suggest you do not spend much time with your teddy bear at all."*

Grammatical, on-topic, and unhelpful. Nothing in SFT told it this is bad, because
**SFT only ever shows the model good examples**. It has been taught what to
imitate, never what to avoid.

**Idea: collect preference pairs and train the model on them.** For the same
prompt, obtain a worse response and a better response:

- **Rejected:** *"I'd suggest you do not spend much time with your teddy bear at all."*
- **Preferred:** *"Of course! Teddy bears not only make awesome companions for a delightful
  sleep, but can also be great buddies for fun activities. How about you both
  watch a movie together?"*

Now there is a signal in both directions.

### The three reasons given

**1. It is easier to compare than to generate.** Judging that A is better than B
is far cheaper and more reliable for a human than writing an ideal answer from
scratch. Writing an excellent response to a technical question requires
expertise; recognising which of two responses is better requires much less.

**2. Distribution is very important for SFT: it is easy to "mess up".** If your
SFT data is written in a style the model cannot naturally produce, or covers a
distribution unlike real usage, you can degrade the model while appearing to
train it.

**3. It is not scalable: data quality is very important and hard to get.** Every
SFT example is a bespoke piece of writing. Preference labels are cheap by
comparison.

**However** — and the lecture is careful to add this — **"model misbehaving" can
also be a good wake-up call to check SFT data quality.** Preference tuning is not
a cure for bad SFT data; if the model is misbehaving systematically, look
upstream first.

> **Intuition — the deepest reason, stated in terms of the loss.** SFT's
> objective is maximum likelihood on good responses. Maximum likelihood has no
> way to express "not that". It can only ever say "more of this", and the only
> thing that implicitly discourages a bad response is that probability mass is
> conserved. But probability mass is enormous and diffuse; pushing up one good
> answer barely moves a specific bad one. Preference tuning changes the *shape* of
> the objective: it optimises a **relative** quantity, the margin between chosen
> and rejected, which is exactly the thing you actually care about. This is why it
> is a genuinely different training stage rather than more SFT.

## 5.2 Collecting preference data

An observation is a pair `(prompt x, response y)`. Labels come in three shapes:

**Pointwise** — each observation gets an absolute score:

```
Obs 1 → 0.4     Obs 2 → 0.9     Obs 3 → 0.1     Obs 4 → 0.2
```

**Pairwise** — each *pair* of observations gets a comparison:

```
Obs 1 < Obs 2      Obs 1 > Obs 3      Obs 1 > Obs 4      Obs 2 > Obs 3
```

**Listwise** — a full ranking:

```
Obs 2 → 1     Obs 1 → 2     Obs 4 → 3     Obs 3 → 4
```

> **Intuition — why pairwise is the workhorse.** Pointwise scoring asks a human
> "how good is this, from 0 to 1?", and humans are terrible at that. Ratings drift
> over a labelling session, differ wildly between annotators, and cluster on
> whatever part of the scale a person finds comfortable. Pairwise comparison asks
> only "which of these two is better?", which is a far more stable judgement and
> requires no shared notion of what 0.7 means. Listwise carries more information
> per annotation but the cognitive load grows fast, and it can be decomposed into
> pairs anyway. Everything downstream in this lecture — Bradley–Terry, reward
> models, DPO — is built on pairwise data.

### The recipe for pairwise data

**Step 1 — generate a pair of responses `(y₁, y₂)` for the same prompt.**

- **Input `x`** — taken from production **logs** or a chosen reference
  distribution.

- **Output `y`** — generated by the **SFT model with `T > 0`** (so the two
  samples differ), or produced synthetically, or written as **rewrites** of an
  existing response.

**Step 2 — label which is preferred, giving `y_w` (winner) and `y_l` (loser).**

- **human rating**,
- **proxies** — LLM-as-a-judge (Lecture 8), BLEU, ROUGE, etc.,
- **variants** — a binary scale (better/worse) versus a more nuanced scale.

> **Watch out — the prompts must come from the real distribution.** If you collect
> preferences on prompts nobody actually asks, you will align the model on a
> distribution it never encounters. This is why "input via logs" is listed first.
> Equally, the responses must come from **the model you are about to train**
> (on-policy), not from some other model: preference tuning teaches the model to
> prefer one of *its own* plausible outputs over another, and off-policy data
> asks it to move towards behaviour it cannot produce.

## 5.3 Reinforcement learning, in language-model clothing

The standard RL diagram has an **agent** taking an **action** in an
**environment**, observing a **state** and receiving a **reward**, according to a
**policy**. The lecture maps each term onto language modelling:

| RL concept | LLM equivalent |
|---|---|
| Agent | the **LLM** |
| Environment | **tokens** |
| State | the **input so far** (the prompt plus what has been generated) |
| Action | the **next token** |
| Policy `π_θ` | the **probability of the next token** given the state |
| Reward | **human preference** |

**The goal: learn `θ` so that `π_θ` aligns with human preferences.**

> **Intuition — and why this framing is *necessary*, not just cute.** Consider
> what makes a response good: it is a property of the *whole* response. You cannot
> label token 7 as correct or incorrect. Supervised learning needs a per-token
> target; here the only signal is a single scalar attached to a complete
> generation. That is precisely the setting RL was invented for — sparse,
> delayed, trajectory-level reward with no per-step supervision. The policy is
> the model, an episode is one generated response, and the reward arrives at the
> end. Note the unusual features of this particular RL problem: the environment is
> deterministic and fully known (appending a token to a string), the action space
> is huge (`V` ≈ 100,000), and episodes are short (hundreds of steps). That
> combination is what makes the specialised algorithms below work.

## 5.4 RLHF

**RLHF = Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback** (Ouyang et al., 2022,
*Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback* — the
InstructGPT paper). Two steps:

- **Step 1 — Reward modelling: distinguish good from bad.**
  Input `(prompt x, response y)`; output a **quantitative score**.

- **Step 2 — Reinforcement learning: align the model.**
  Input a prompt; output a response, optimised against that score.

### Step 1: reward modelling

**Idea: know which answers are bad and which are good, via a Reward Model (RM).**

Given the prompt *"Suggest a new activity I could do with my teddy bear"*, the RM
scores each candidate response — high for the enthusiastic movie suggestion, low
for *"I'd suggest you do not spend much time with your teddy bear at all."*

**The Bradley–Terry formulation** (Bradley & Terry, 1952, *The rank analysis of
incomplete block designs*). The probability that `y_w` is preferred to `y_l`:

```
P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = exp(r_w) / (exp(r_w) + exp(r_l))
                 = σ(r_w − r_l)
```

where `r_w = r(x, y_w)` and `r_l = r(x, y_l)` are the rewards of the two
responses and `σ` is the logistic sigmoid.

**Training.** Learn `r` by maximum likelihood on the pairwise preference data,
i.e. minimise:

```
L_RM = − E_{(x, y_w, y_l)} [ log σ( r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l) ) ]
```

**Data:**

- `O(10,000)` observations,
- the label is a **human rating** — *which is where the "HF" in RLHF comes from*.

**Model:**

- a **pretrained LLM with a classification head** (a scalar output head) instead
  of the next-token-prediction head,

- or **encoder-only**: BERT and the like, via a `[CLS]` projection.

*(Evaluation of reward models: RewardBench, Lambert et al., 2024.)*

> **Intuition — Bradley–Terry is the bridge from comparisons to numbers, and it
> is worth understanding why it is the natural choice.** You have relative data
> (A beat B) and you want an absolute scalar function. Bradley–Terry posits a
> latent "strength" per item such that the probability of A beating B depends only
> on the *difference* in strengths, through a sigmoid. This is exactly the model
> behind Elo ratings in chess. Two consequences follow.
>
> First, the reward is only identified **up to an additive constant** — adding 5 to
> every reward changes no preference probability. So absolute reward values are
> meaningless; only differences mean anything. That is why you should never read
> "reward = 0.8" as a quality score.
>
> Second, notice the loss is just logistic regression on the reward *difference*.
> Which means the RM is not learning "how good is this response" — it is learning
> "which of two responses a human would pick", and that is all it can be trusted
> to do. Push it far outside the range of differences it saw in training and it
> extrapolates badly, which is precisely the reward-hacking failure below.

### Step 2: reinforcement learning

**Idea: change the weights of the LLM to penalise bad answers and promote good
answers, via RL using the reward model.**

The loop: the LLM (the **policy**, *trained*) receives a prompt and generates
a response; the RM (*frozen*) scores it; the score is used to update the
policy's weights via RL.

**Data:**

- `O(100,000)` observations,
- the label is **the score given by the reward model** — note that no human is in
  this loop; humans were only needed for the 10,000 examples that trained the RM.

**Model:** initialised at the SFT model.

**Training — the objective function:**

```
maximise  E_{x, y~π_θ} [ r(x, y) ]  −  β · KL( π_θ(y|x) ‖ π_ref(y|x) )
          └── maximise rewards ──┘     └─ don't deviate too much from base ─┘
```

The KL term exists to **avoid "reward hacking" and training instability.**

> **Intuition — the KL term is the most important part of the objective, and the
> reason is worth spelling out.** The reward model is an imperfect approximation
> of human preference, trained on a specific distribution of responses. If you
> optimise against it without restraint, the policy will find the places where the
> approximation breaks. This is **reward hacking**, and in practice it produces
> memorable pathologies: models that emit the same flattering phrase repeatedly,
> or degenerate into a fixed high-scoring string, because the RM never saw such
> text and scores it absurdly highly. It is Goodhart's Law made mechanical — a
> theme Lecture 8 returns to by name.
>
> The KL penalty says: stay in the region where the reward model is trustworthy,
> namely near the distribution its training data came from. It is a **trust
> region**, in the literal sense. The coefficient `β` is the central knob of RLHF:
> too small and the model hacks the reward and loses its language ability; too
> large and nothing changes. A secondary benefit is preventing catastrophic
> forgetting — the KL anchor keeps everything the model learned in pretraining and
> SFT, since drifting away from `π_ref` is penalised everywhere, not only where
> the reward is high.

### PPO

**PPO = Proximal Policy Optimisation** (Schulman et al., 2017), the algorithm
used to actually perform that optimisation.

**PPO computes advantages, not just rewards.**

```
maximise  E[ A ]  −  β · KL(...)
          └ maximise advantages ┘

Advantage ≈ Reward − Baseline,   where the baseline is a Value function
```

**The value function:**

- **token-level**,
- answers "what would the reward be if I follow the policy from here?",
- **trained jointly with the policy**,
- its label is the reward.

*(Suggested reading: Schulman et al., 2015, on **GAE** — Generalised Advantage
Estimation — the standard method for computing advantages.)*

> **Intuition — why subtract a baseline?** The raw reward tells you how good an
> outcome was, but not whether your *action* deserves credit for it. Suppose every
> response to a particular easy prompt scores around 0.9. A policy update
> proportional to raw reward would reinforce all of them strongly, including the
> mediocre ones, purely because the prompt was easy. Subtracting the expected
> reward for that state isolates the part attributable to the choice: "this
> response was 0.05 better than what I'd typically produce here". That subtraction
> leaves the *direction* of the gradient unbiased while dramatically reducing its
> **variance** — and variance is what makes policy-gradient methods fail to
> converge. Keep this in mind, because **GRPO in Lecture 6 is exactly this idea
> with a cheaper baseline**: instead of training a value network to predict the
> expected reward, just generate a group of responses and use their average.

### Variation 1: PPO-Clip

**Idea: clip the ratio between the new and old policy to prevent large updates.**

```
ratio:  r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t | s_t) / π_θ_old(a_t | s_t)

objective:  E[ min( r_t(θ)·A_t ,  clip(r_t(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε)·A_t ) ]
```

Two terminology warnings that the lecture makes explicitly:

> **Watch out (1).** This is **confusing because it is an objective function
> ("maximise") and NOT a loss.** You maximise it. Implementations negate it to
> feed into a minimiser, which is a routine source of sign errors.

<!-- -->

> **Watch out (2).** It is **confusing because rewards are conventionally written
> `r`.** Here `r_t(θ)` is the **probability ratio**, not a reward. Two different
> `r`s in the same equation.

<!-- -->

> **Intuition — what clipping does, geometrically.** The ratio measures how much
> more (or less) likely the new policy makes an action that the old policy took.
> If a token had a positive advantage, gradient ascent wants to raise its
> probability — but raising it by a factor of 50 in one step would move the policy
> far outside the region where the collected data (and the estimated advantages)
> are still valid. The `min` with the clipped term removes the *incentive* to go
> beyond `1 ± ε`: once the ratio leaves that band in the improving direction, the
> objective flattens and the gradient vanishes. Note the asymmetry created by the
> `min` — it caps the upside of moving too far but does *not* cap the penalty for
> a bad action, so PPO remains free to move away from mistakes quickly. This
> "proximal" clipping is what makes PPO stable enough to use without the
> second-order machinery of its predecessor TRPO, which is why it became the
> default.

### Variation 2: PPO-KL Penalty

**Idea: penalise the difference in policy distributions** — add
`−β·KL(π_θ ‖ π_something)` to the objective rather than clipping the ratio.

**Terminology:**

- **old** = the model from the previous RL iteration,
- **ref** = the base model.

**Nowadays, the KL divergence is taken with respect to `ref` (the base model).**

> **Intuition — and why "ref" not "old" is the right choice.** KL against the
> *old* policy is a step-size control: it stops any single update being too large,
> but the policy can still wander arbitrarily far over many small steps.
> KL against the *reference* (base) model is an **anchor**: it bounds the total
> distance travelled from the starting point, no matter how many steps you take.
> Since the failure mode we actually fear is cumulative drift into
> reward-hacking territory, anchoring to `ref` is what you want. In practice
> modern implementations use both mechanisms: clip for step-size control,
> KL-to-ref as the anchor.

### Alternatives to PPO

**Limitations of PPO:**

- **it needs 4 models** — the policy, the value model, the reward model, and the
  base (reference) model,

- **is it worth it?**

**Variants:** REINFORCE, **GRPO** (DeepSeekMath, Shao et al., 2024), and many
more.

> **Intuition — count the memory.** For a 70B policy you need: the policy being
> trained (weights + gradients + optimiser state), a value model of comparable
> size (also trained), a frozen reward model, and a frozen reference copy. That is
> roughly four models resident simultaneously, only two of them contributing
> parameters you keep. The engineering burden is the real reason both GRPO
> (drop the value model) and DPO (drop the reward model *and* the value model)
> exist.

### Challenges with the RL-based approach

The full list from the lecture, which reads as a summary of everyone's
frustration with RLHF:

- requires **training a reward model** — a 2-stage process,
- **many hyperparameters** to tune,
- **training instability**,
- unclear **metric to monitor training** (reward going up may just mean hacking),
- **need diversity in completions** — if the policy collapses to one style there
  is nothing to learn from,

- **not abundantly clear why preference tuning absolutely needs RL**.

That last point is the direct set-up for DPO.

## 5.5 Best-of-N: skipping RL entirely

**BoN = Best of N.** **Idea: skip the RL step and just use the reward model's
scores.**

**Strategy:**

- given a prompt, generate several outputs with the SFT model,
- rank the outputs by the score given by the reward model,
- take the best one.

The lecture's worked example. Prompt: *"Suggest a new activity I could do with my
teddy bear."* Three sampled responses, scored by the RM:

| Response | RM score | Rank |
|---|---|---|
| *"Take your teddy bear on a picnic in your backyard."* | 0.8 | 1 |
| *"Of course! Teddy bears not only make awesome companions... watch a movie together?"* | 0.2 | 2 |
| *"I'd suggest you do not spend much time with your teddy bear at all."* | −2 | 3 |

Output the rank-1 response.

> **Intuition — a strong baseline that is easy to underrate.** BoN requires no
> training whatsoever beyond the reward model you already have, and it is
> frequently *competitive with full RLHF* on quality. That is a slightly
> uncomfortable fact and it is why the lecture presents it before DPO. The cost
> has simply been moved: RLHF pays once at training time and then serves cheaply;
> BoN pays `N×` on **every single inference call, forever**. Note the family
> resemblance to self-consistency from Lecture 3 — generate many, pick one — with
> a learned reward model doing the picking instead of a majority vote. Also note
> that BoN is vulnerable to the same reward hacking as RLHF, and more so as `N`
> grows: sampling 1000 candidates and taking the maximum is an efficient search
> for the RM's blind spots.

## 5.6 DPO

### Motivation

- the limitations of using RL (four models, instability, hyperparameters),
- **Best-of-N is costly at inference time**,
- **why don't we train in a supervised fashion?**

### The result

**DPO = Direct Preference Optimisation** (Rafailov et al., 2023, *Your Language
Model is Secretly a Reward Model*).

**Rewrite the loss function in a supervised way:**

```
L_DPO = − E_{(x, y_w, y_l)} [ log σ(  β·log( π_θ(y_w|x) / π_ref(y_w|x) )
                                    − β·log( π_θ(y_l|x) / π_ref(y_l|x) ) ) ]
```

**Properties:**

- **no need to train a separate reward model** — there is no `r(x, y)` anywhere
  in the loss,

- it **operates directly on preference data**,
- it is **similar to the Bradley–Terry formulation, with a special kind of
  reward**.

### Where the formulation comes from

The lecture derives it in five steps, and the derivation is the point of the
section:

**1. Start from the PPO objective:**

```
max_π  E_{x, y~π} [ r(x,y) ]  −  β·KL( π(y|x) ‖ π_ref(y|x) )
```

**2. Derive the optimal policy.** This constrained problem has a closed-form
solution — a standard result for KL-regularised reward maximisation:

```
π*(y|x) = (1/Z(x)) · π_ref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β )
```

where `Z(x)` is the normalising partition function.

**3. Identify a "reward" term.** Rearranging the above for `r`:

```
r(x,y) = β · log( π*(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) )  +  β·log Z(x)
```

So the reward is recoverable from the optimal policy — *the language model is
secretly a reward model*, hence the paper's title.

**4. Write the Bradley–Terry formulation for this "reward".** Substituting into
`P(y_w ≻ y_l) = σ(r_w − r_l)`:

```
P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = σ(  β·log(π*(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x))
                     − β·log(π*(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x)) )
```

The crucial cancellation: **`β·log Z(x)` appears in both terms and cancels in
the difference.** The intractable partition function disappears.

**5. "Infer" the DPO loss function.** Replace the unknown optimal policy `π*`
with the policy you are training, `π_θ`, and maximise the likelihood of the
observed preferences. That is exactly `L_DPO` above.

> **Intuition — what the derivation really shows.** RLHF is a two-step pipeline:
> fit a reward model to preferences, then find the policy that maximises it under
> a KL constraint. DPO observes that step 2 has a *closed-form* answer, so the
> composition of the two steps can be written as a single function of the policy.
> The reward model was never necessary as an explicit object — it was an
> intermediate variable that can be eliminated algebraically. What you are left
> with is a classification loss over preference pairs, trainable with ordinary
> supervised learning: two forward passes on the policy, two on the frozen
> reference, one sigmoid, done.
>
> Read the loss to see what it actually does. The term
> `β·log(π_θ(y_w)/π_ref(y_w))` is the *implicit reward* of the winner: how much
> more likely the current policy makes it than the base model did. The loss
> pushes the winner's implicit reward above the loser's. So DPO increases the
> **margin** between chosen and rejected — not the absolute likelihood of either.
> This matters in practice: it is entirely normal for the log-probability of the
> *chosen* response to go **down** during DPO training, as long as the loser's
> goes down faster. A well-known failure mode is both dropping too far, which
> degrades fluency; that is what the `β` and the reference anchor are for.

### RLHF or DPO?

From *Is DPO Superior to PPO for LLM Alignment? A Comprehensive Study*
(Xu et al., 2024):

| | RLHF (PPO) | DPO |
|---|---|---|
| Training | multi-stage training | supervised learning |
| Extra models needed | reward model, value model, base model | **base model only** |

**Ease of implementation:** DPO, clearly.

**Performance: no absolute consensus. It varies from task to task and is
sensitive to implementation.**

> **Intuition — the honest state of the field, and the reason for the split.**
> DPO wins on simplicity by a wide margin and is what you should reach for first.
> But well-tuned PPO-based RLHF often edges it out at the frontier, and there is a
> structural reason: PPO is **on-policy** — it generates fresh responses from the
> current policy, scores them, and learns from its own present behaviour. DPO
> learns from a **fixed, offline** dataset of pairs collected from some earlier
> model. As DPO training proceeds, the policy drifts away from the distribution
> its preference data came from, and the data becomes progressively less relevant
> to what the model now does. This is why a whole family of successors exists
> (iterative DPO, online DPO, IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO), most of which are attempts
> to reintroduce on-policy freshness without reintroducing the four-model
> apparatus.

### The result, end to end

Tracking the same question through all three stages:

- **Pretrained only:** *"Can I put my teddy bear in the washer?"* →
  *"Teddy bears are often made of materials like polyester and cotton, with
  plastic eyes and sometimes small accessories."* (a continuation, not an answer)

- **+ instruction tuned:** → *"No, it might get damaged. Try hand washing
  instead."* (answers the question)

- **+ preference tuned:** → *"It's better not to. Your teddy could get hurt! A
  gentle hand wash is safer."* (answers the question in a tone people preferred)

> **Intuition — what each stage bought you.** Pretraining gave **knowledge**
> (it knows what teddy bears are made of). SFT gave **format** (a question gets
> an answer). Preference tuning gave **style and judgement** (warm, concise,
> appropriately cautious). All three examples are factually consistent — the
> difference between them is not knowledge but behaviour, which is exactly why
> alignment is a separate problem from capability.
